III. A Winter of Letters

It did not happen that night, nor the next. The ferryman explained that the light could only be fetched when the river had gone back down to its ordinary level, and that would take weeks. In the meantime, he said, she should go home and light the other twelve lamps as usual, and read.

"Read what?" Ada asked.

"Everything your family wrote down. They were careful people. They'll have left you instructions, even if they didn't know they were doing it."

So that winter Ada read. She brought the tin box down from the attic and set it on the kitchen table, and in the evenings, when the lamps were lit and the supper things washed, she took out the almanacs one at a time and went through them page by page. Most of what she found was what she had always expected: sunset and sunrise, wind and rain, a broken mantle here, a stolen ladder there. But between the ordinary lines there were other things, written in the margins or on the inside covers, that she had never noticed before.

Her great-grandmother, it turned out, had kept bees, and had written down the weight of honey she took from the hives each August. Her grandfather had courted her grandmother for three years before she agreed to marry him, and had recorded each refusal with a small, sad cross in the margin. Her father had kept a list of books he meant to read, and had crossed off fewer than half of them. Ada copied out the rest of the list on a clean sheet of paper and pinned it above the stove.

She also found letters. They were tucked between the pages of the almanacs, folded small, and most of them were addressed to the lamplighter who would come after. The river is not unkind, one of them said, in a hand she did not recognise. It only keeps what it is given. Do not give it more than it asks for, and do not let it keep what is yours. Another one, from her grandfather, said simply: Take a warm coat. It is colder down there than you would think.

The one from her father was the shortest of all. It was written on the back of a receipt from the ironmonger's, and it was dated the week before he died. Ada, it said, I should have told you about this, and I am sorry I did not. I kept meaning to wait until you were ready, and then I kept meaning to wait until I was. The ferryman will show you what to do. It is not difficult, only strange. You will be good at it. You are good at most things, though I don't think I said so often enough. Love, Dad.

She read it several times, and then she put it in the pocket of her coat, where it stayed for the rest of the winter.

In January the river froze along its edges for the first time in twenty years. Children slid on the ice under the bridge until the constable chased them off, and the brickworks closed for a week because the clay was too hard to dig. Ada lit her twelve lamps every evening and watched the thirteenth stay dark. People had begun to notice. Mrs Penhallow from the chapel asked her about it after the Sunday service, and Ada said it was a fault in the main and that the gas company had been informed, which was not exactly a lie.

At the end of February the ice broke up and went down to the sea in great grey plates that knocked against the piles of the bridge all night. The next morning there was a note pushed under Ada's door. It was written on a page torn from the green ledger, and it said: Tonight. Bring the key and the taper and wear the coat.
