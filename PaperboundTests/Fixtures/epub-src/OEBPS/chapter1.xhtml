I. The Lamp on Harrow Street

Every evening at a quarter past six, Ada Marsh climbed the iron ladder that leaned against the last gas lamp on Harrow Street, and every evening she paused on the fourth rung to look down the hill at the town. From there she could see the chimneys of the brickworks, the slate roofs of the chapel, the long grey back of the river, and the twelve lamps she had already lit, each one a small promise kept against the dark.

She had been the lamplighter for nine years. Her father had held the post for thirty before her, and his father before him, and somewhere in the attic of the narrow house at the bottom of the street there was a tin box of almanacs going back further than anyone could remember. Each one was a cheap paper book, sewn with linen thread, and each one recorded the same things in the same cramped hand: the hour of sunset, the state of the weather, the number of lamps lit, and the number that would not light.

Ada had never thought the almanacs interesting. They were simply part of the work, like the brass key that opened the lamp housings or the long pole with its hooked end that she used on windy nights when the ladder would not stand. But on the evening this story begins, the lamp at the top of Harrow Street would not light, and that was the first time in nine years it had happened.

She tried the valve twice. She cleaned the mantle with the corner of her sleeve and tried again. The gas hissed, faint and steady, and the little flame from her taper leaned toward it as if it wanted very much to catch. It did not catch. The glass housing stayed dark, and in the dark glass Ada saw her own face, pale and puzzled, and behind it the lights of the town going on one by one as people came home and drew their curtains.

She climbed down, folded the ladder, and stood for a while in the gathering cold. It was the kind of October evening that smells of leaves and coal smoke and, faintly, of the river. A dog barked somewhere near the chapel. A cart went by at the bottom of the hill with its own lantern swinging. Nobody else came up Harrow Street, because nobody ever did after dark; the street ended at the old wall of the cemetery, and beyond the wall there was only the path down to the ferry, which had not run since the bridge was built.

When she got home she went up to the attic, which she had not done since her father died, and she took down the tin box and opened it on the floor. The almanacs smelled of damp and pipe tobacco. She found the one for the year she was born and turned the pages until she reached October. There, in her grandfather's hand, under the date and the hour of sunset, were the words she had half expected and still did not want to read: Thirteen lit. Harrow top would not light. Went down to the ferry.

She sat back on her heels. Below her the house creaked as it settled for the night. She turned to the almanac for the following October, and the one after, and found nothing unusual; thirteen lamps lit, every evening, the weather fair or foul. Then, eleven years later, there it was again: Harrow top would not light. Went down to the ferry. And eleven years after that, in her father's handwriting this time, smaller and more careful: Harrow top dark. I went to the ferry as he told me. It is as he said.

As he said. Ada read the line several times. Her father had never mentioned the ferry. He had never mentioned anything about the lamp at the top of the street except that its housing needed new putty every spring and that the ladder was not to be leaned on the left side, where the wall was soft. She thought about the cemetery path, the steep wet steps, the landing stage where the ferry used to tie up, and the black water beyond it. She thought about going to bed.

Instead she put on her coat again, took the hand lamp from its hook by the door, and went out.
