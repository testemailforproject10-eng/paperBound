II. What the River Kept

The steps down to the ferry were worse than she remembered. Moss had grown over the edges and the iron rail had rusted through in two places, so that she had to hold the hand lamp out in front of her and feel for each step with the toe of her boot. The river was loud. It had rained in the hills all week, and the water came down brown and fast and full of branches, talking to itself in the dark.

At the bottom there was the landing stage, a platform of tarred planks on wooden piles, and at the end of the landing stage there was a hut. Ada had seen it from the bridge a thousand times without thinking about it. It had a tin roof and one small window, and the window, she saw now, was lit.

She knocked. After a moment the door was opened by an old man in a fisherman's jersey, with a face so creased and brown it looked as though it had been folded up and kept in a pocket for years. He looked at her, and at the hand lamp, and then up the hill toward Harrow Street, where the top lamp was still dark.

"Marsh," he said. It was not a question. "You'll be the daughter. Come in, then. You're letting the warm out."

Inside, the hut was a single room with a stove, a table, two chairs, and a great many shelves. The shelves were full of jars, and in the jars, Ada saw as her eyes got used to the light, there were small things the river had brought down: buttons, keys, a child's tin soldier, spectacles, rings, a pocket watch with the glass broken, a doll's shoe. Each jar had a paper label tied to it with string, and each label had a date.*

"My father came here," Ada said.

"He did. Every eleven years, regular as a clock, when the lamp went dark. And his father before him." The old man pulled out a chair for her and put the kettle on the stove. "I'm the ferryman. Not that there's a ferry any more. But somebody has to keep the ledger, and somebody has to keep the lamp, and the two of us have to meet once in a while to make sure the sums come out."

The Ferryman's Ledger

The ledger was a heavy book bound in green cloth, so old that the cloth had worn through at the corners and the boards showed underneath. The ferryman laid it on the table between them and opened it to a page near the end. The columns were ruled in red ink, and the handwriting changed every few pages, as if the book had passed through many owners.

"Everything the river takes," he said, "it gives back, sooner or later. Most of it comes back down here, to the landing stage, and I fish it out and write it down and keep it until somebody comes asking. That's the easy part. The hard part is the things that don't come back on their own. Those have to be fetched."

"Fetched from where?"

He did not answer straight away. He poured the tea, which was black and very strong, and pushed a cup across the table to her. Then he turned the ledger around so that she could read it, and put one thick finger on a line near the bottom of the page. It said, in a hand she now knew well because she had been reading it all evening: One lamp's worth of light, owed to Harrow Street. To be fetched by the lamplighter, as before.

"Light," Ada said. "The river took the light?"

"Every eleven years the water comes up high enough to reach the bottom of the cemetery wall, where the old gas main runs. It's only for a night, and nobody notices. But it takes a little of the light with it, the way it takes everything, and the top lamp is the first to go dark. If nobody fetches it back, the next lamp goes the year after, and the one after that, until the whole street is dark and nobody can remember why." He drank some of his tea. "Your family has been fetching it back for a hundred and forty years. You're the next one. That's all."

Ada looked down at the ledger, and at the cup in her hands, and at the jars on the shelves with their small, patient labels. Outside, the river went on talking. She found that she was not frightened, only very tired, and curious in a way she had not been for a long time.

"How do I fetch it?" she said.

* The oldest label Ada could read was dated 1791. The jar it belonged to held a single brass button, very green, and the label said only Returned. Not yet claimed.
