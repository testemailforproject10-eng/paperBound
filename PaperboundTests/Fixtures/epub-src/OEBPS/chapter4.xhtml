IV. The Last Page

The ferryman was waiting for her on the landing stage, and tied to the end of it, where Ada was quite sure there had been nothing the night before, was a small black boat with a lantern hung from a pole at the stern. The lantern was not lit.

"You light it," the ferryman said. "That's the whole of the trick, really. You light the lantern, and you row out to the middle, and you wait. When the light comes up to meet you, you bring it home."

"How will I know it?"

"You'll know it," he said. "It'll look like your street."

She climbed into the boat. It was steadier than she expected, and the oars were worn smooth and pale where other hands had held them. She opened the lantern with her brass key, struck a match, and lit the taper, and then she lit the wick, and the little flame stood up straight and yellow in the still air. The ferryman untied the rope and pushed her off with his boot, and the current took the boat gently and turned it toward the middle of the river.

It was very quiet out there. The water, which had been so loud all winter, had gone smooth and black, and the lights of the town lay on it in long trembling lines. Ada shipped the oars and sat with her hands in her lap and waited. She thought about her father, and about the letter in her pocket, and about the list of books above the stove. She thought that she would read the first one tomorrow. It was a book about the stars.

After a while she noticed that one of the reflections on the water was not moving with the others. It was a small, round, warm light, a little below the surface, and it was coming slowly toward the boat. As it came closer she saw that it was not one light but many, very small, like the lit windows of a street seen from a long way off: a chapel, a row of narrow houses, a cemetery wall, and at the top of a hill, one lamp.

She leaned over the side of the boat and held the lantern out above the water, the way she held the taper up to the lamps on Harrow Street. The light rose to meet it. For a moment the whole river shone, and then the light went into the lantern and the flame in the lantern grew tall and bright, and the river was dark again.

She rowed back. The ferryman helped her onto the landing stage and took the lantern from her and looked at it for a long moment, and then he gave it back.

"Go on, then," he said. "It's your street."

Ada climbed the steps with the lantern in her hand, past the cemetery wall and up onto Harrow Street, and set her ladder against the last lamp, leaning it on the right side where the wall was sound. She climbed to the fourth rung and paused, the way she always did, and looked down at the town. Then she opened the housing and held the lantern to the mantle, and the thirteenth lamp on Harrow Street caught, and burned, and stayed lit.

When she got home she took a new almanac from the drawer, a cheap paper book sewn with linen thread, and on the first page, under the date and the hour of sunset, she wrote: Thirteen lit. Went down to the ferry. It is as they said.

And underneath, after a moment, she added: It was not difficult, only strange.
